Game Design

This section records the high-level design vision for The Seeker: who it is for, how it should look and feel, what it means, how it works as an open-source brand, and which mechanics it aims for.

These pages describe intent, not the current build. Some details have changed in the game itself. For example, skills, weapons, and passives come from research and crafting at capsules, and the creature roster differs from the ecosystem sketch. The Architecture pages and the source code describe how the game currently works.

Target Audience

This game combines various features and mechanics of:

  • metroidvanias (i.e Hollow Knight)
  • action RPGs (i.e Diablo)
  • Sandbox Adventure Games (i.e Red Dead Redemption)

The fans of these genres are then naturally the audiences we're targetting. This game is meant to give you the sense of mystery and non-linear exploration known from Metriodvanias, combined with the deep character build customization and intense combat known from action RPGs, while also allowing the player to (for the most part) define their own path and goals while inhabiting a mostly 'natural' and un-scripted world, like in Sandbox Adventures.

Art Direction

  • Low-res pixel art (6x6 tile size, 12px player height)
  • Low saturation
  • No outlines
  • Focus on good readability and color harmony
  • Player should ideally be the only yellow'y asset in game to make it very visible
  • All assets drawn fully lit and then darkness added as a post processing shader
  • Multi-layered and parallaxed background and foregrounds
  • Focus on auto-tiling, to save time designing levels
  • Backgrounds and foreground mostly simple silhouettes so attention is on the main platforms
  • Procedural fogs and blur layers to build atmosphere and make backgrounds grab even less attention
  • Atmospheric lighting and bloom™️
  • I would love to have lighting with normal maps at some point but it might be too much work
  • All animations play at 12fps
  • Shaders for environment so it reacts to player (like vegetation moves when you run through it, or water splashes etc)
  • Procedural 'dust' trail behind player when jumping (like in hollow knight)

Lore

The Seeker is a science-fiction universe about curiosity, transformation, mathematics, and cosmic horror, and about what happens to someone who wants to experience everything.

This page sketches the world and the questions it explores. It deliberately leaves out the story's twists and endings. They belong to the game, and every other work set in this universe is free to find its own answers.

The Seekers

Seekers are explorers in the service of Theorem, a vast organization devoted to discovering and formalizing knowledge across the universe. Seeker is a role, not a species: humans, aliens, and machines alike can become one. The seekers of SEEKER NET, for example, are tiny, computer-like robots.

What Seekers chase are permutations of existence. Seeing ultraviolet light is one kind of existence. Thinking as a swarm is another, and so is living beneath an ocean of ice. Combine them and you get something no being has felt before: a swarm-mind drifting under the ice, perceiving colors no eye has ever seen. Seekers crave these combinations. They have already found the familiar ingredients, so they travel ever farther in search of strange ones, the kind that make every earlier combination feel small.

A few ideas shape the life of a Seeker:

  • The Codex is Theorem's shared archive of everything its Seekers have recorded. A Seeker reaches it through their visor to scan, record, and analyze what they find.
  • Attunement is the ability to sense the frequencies and structures of other beings and of the cosmos itself. Seekers learn it through training, collaboration, and meditation before setting out alone.
  • Solitude. Seekers travel and work alone, far from the organization that sent them.

The planet

On a lonely expedition, a Seeker feels a pull unlike anything before and follows it to an icy planet whose richness seems almost too perfect.

The surface is frozen and exposed. Life shelters in caves beneath the ice, warmed by the planet's heat. The deeper you go, the warmer it gets and the stranger, more beautiful, and more dangerous the ecosystems become. Others have come here too, scientists, opportunists, caretakers, and worshippers among them, each with their own idea of what the planet is for.

Something near the planet's core seems to be calling. What it is, and why it calls, is the heart of the story.

Ancient alien demigods called Guardians watch over the planet. Their origins are a mystery.

What the story explores

The Seeker asks questions more than it answers them:

  • The pull of novelty. What draws a Seeker onward, and does the pursuit of new experiences ever end? What would "enough" mean to someone who has already tasted everything ordinary?
  • Expansion and simplicity. One path leads toward more: more knowledge, abilities, power, and possibility. The other leads toward less: humility, service, and letting go. Both should feel genuinely appealing, and neither is presented as obviously right.
  • Recording versus experiencing. Is recording something in the Codex the same as understanding it? Is understanding a creature the same as becoming it?
  • Who benefits. Who gains from the knowledge Theorem gathers, and at what cost?
  • The planet's nature. Is its richness a gift, a call, an accident, or something with no human analogue?

Permutations in play

The themes are meant to be felt through play as well as story. The game aims to let players learn the abilities and even the forms of the creatures they meet. Creatures, in turn, adapt to how the player fights, and the caves themselves shift and change. Each discovery opens the way to the next, so the world responds to how it is explored.

Mathematics as experience

The deepest layer of The Seeker is inspired by the idea that mathematics could be the foundation of reality, consciousness, and even emotion. A triangle can be defined as three connected points, but it can also be an experience, just as red feels different from green and one musical interval feels different from another. The aim is to let players experience mathematical relationships rather than only understand them: how primes feel compared with even numbers, or how a Fibonacci sequence feels compared with a fractal.

Everything is tuned around one fundamental number, 12:

  • Standard animations run at 12 frames per second.
  • Music is built on a 48 Hz fundamental, two octaves above the animation rate. Every pitch is 48 Hz times a ratio whose denominator is a power of two, so every note belongs to the fundamental's harmonic series. See Music and Tuning.
  • The rhythm is 90 BPM, the same fundamental several octaves lower.
  • Tiles are 6×6 pixels, and the player is 12 pixels tall.

The result is meant to feel resonant and meditative. Players aren't told about it; at best it simply feels good. The player becomes an instrument too: steps, strikes, and impacts land on the shared musical grid. From that foundation, levels, music, and motion can explore different mathematical ideas, such as platforms sized in prime multiples or melodies built from a sequence, and combine them in unexpected ways.

Influences

The universe draws on ideas from mathematics, science, and philosophy, used as inspiration rather than doctrine:

  • Kurt Gödel: the limits of formal systems and the infinite variety of what remains to be discovered.
  • Donald Hoffman: the idea that perception is an interface over a deeper structure, and that experience itself has mathematical structure.
  • Stephen Wolfram's ruliad: the space of all possible computations, endlessly branching.
  • Contemplative traditions: humility, service, and the freedom of letting go.

Open questions for creators

These questions are left open on purpose. Any game, story, campaign, or artwork set in this universe can answer them in its own way:

  • What does the planet want, if it wants anything?
  • Can a Seeker stop seeking?
  • When does structure support life, and when does it become a cage?
  • What do the planet's inhabitants know that Theorem doesn't?

None of this is required. The lore is an open invitation, not a rulebook, so take from it whatever you like and leave the rest. You don't have to explore these questions or make anything resembling a metroidvania. Build a racing game across the icy surface, a cozy farming sim in the warm caves, a puzzle game about the Codex, or something no one has thought of yet. Borrow a single idea, such as the frozen planet, Theorem, or the 12-based tuning, and build something completely different on top of it.

To build your own work in this universe, see Open Brand.

Open Brand

The Seeker is an open-source brand: a shared sci-fi universe that anyone can play in, build on, and take in their own direction. The game in this repository, headed for a Steam release, is the trunk of the tree. The branches can be anything: other games, art projects, 3D worlds, music, prints, merch.

The brand isn't owned by a company, or even by us. Its code, art, audio, and lore are open to everyone. Ownership is cultural, and it lives in our collectibles. Holders are the game masters of this shared world. Think of the Gamemakers in The Hunger Games, reshaping the arena while everyone else plays, minus the fight to the death. Holders help decide how the planet changes, which guardians watch over it, and where its story goes next. Anyone can play without owning anything; holders get to shape the world everyone plays in.

Why open source

AI makes it possible to rebuild almost any product from a screenshot and a prompt. Copyright still exists, but when anyone can recreate a game in an afternoon, a creative project can't depend on it anymore. We saw this coming, and The Seeker has been open source from day one.

So instead of fighting copies, we invite them. Open source spreads on its own: every fork, remix, and spin-off brings The Seeker's world to new people, and the best ideas can flow back into the original.

Owning a piece of the brand

Open source generates attention and culture, but on its own it gives the people who grow a brand no share in what it becomes. Collectibles fix that. Think of the first trading cards of a famous franchise: the bigger the world gets, the more they matter. Our collectibles work the same way for everything built on The Seeker.

  • You help run the world. Holders are the game masters. They're planned to make real-time changes to the game world, place and customize characters on the planet, and steer how it evolves. Today they share a private chat with the team where suggestions shape the game, along with early releases and giveaways.
  • The history is on-chain. We've been inscribing The Seeker's development on Bitcoin since 2023, starting with some of the first ten thousand Ordinals ever made. That timestamped record predates today's AI tools, and every version of The Seeker, whoever builds it, traces back to it.
  • The game itself stays free of monetization. It's open source, playable without owning anything, and never pay-to-win. Collectibles capture the attention the brand earns instead of charging players for it.
  • Design stays honest. Because the game isn't what's being sold, design decisions are made for the game, not for profit. There are no publishers or middlemen in between, and anyone can contribute as much or as little as they want.

Collectibles are art and membership in the community. They aren't an investment product, and nothing here promises financial returns.

Collections

SEEKER NET (Robinhood Chain)

SEEKER NET brings the world to a new chain. It's a complete game and a social, economic, and art experiment of its own: 2,222 fully animated seekers charge toward activation, and the path each one takes becomes its final art.

  • Every seeker below 9,000 charge gains 50 charge a day. At 9,000 it activates.
  • Moving a seeker (a sale, a gift, or a transfer between wallets) releases a quarter of its charge, split evenly among every other seeker still charging. Every move makes everyone else stronger.
  • Discharge is a seeker's latest release, and Uplink is everything it has ever given to the network.
  • There are two quests. The main quest is charge: hold until activation. The side quest is Discharge and Uplink: power the network on the way there. No strategy is best, there are only different paths, and the rarest seekers will be the ones that took the paths fewer people chose.
  • No new NFTs will be minted. Instead, top pixel artists keep adding traits to the existing seekers, until every decision, community participation included, shows up in the art.

The Seeker (Ordinals)

The Seeker is the game's development diary, inscribed as it happens. Many of its first pieces are in the Sub-10K club, among the first ten thousand inscriptions on Bitcoin.

Planetary Primes (Ordinals)

Planetary Primes are strange mathematical artifacts inscribed on prime-numbered sats, a nod to the mathematical layer of the lore. They come with community access and giveaways, and once the game launches, Primes holders are planned to be able to change the game world in real time.

Planetary Guardians (Ordinals)

Planetary Guardians are alien demigods who will appear on the planet. Holders are planned to be able to customize their guardian in the game and choose where it stands. They also make great profile pictures.

Gameplay and Mechanics

The Seeker combines the exploration and movement of a metroidvania (think Hollow Knight) with the interlocking builds of an action RPG (Diablo, Risk of Rain) and the freedom of a sandbox. Underneath all three runs the idea at the heart of the lore: permutations. Every creature, material, skill, and choice should be able to combine with the others into something new, and the world should respond to how you play.

  • Active Skills: weapons, the skill toolbar, and abilities copied from creatures
  • Passive Skills: equipment that bends your stats and rules
  • Platforming: how the Seeker moves
  • Creatures and Ecosystem: the planet's life and how the planet fights back

Each page separates what the game does today from where it is heading.

The core loop

  1. Descend. Explore procedurally generated caves that grow warmer, stranger, and more dangerous with depth, from icy upper caves into tundra below.
  2. Fight, copy, and gather. Copy the abilities of the creatures you meet, defeat them for Essence and drops, and harvest plants, berries, and ore from the caves.
  3. Return to a capsule. Capsules are your shelter. Store materials safely, spend Essence to research new recipes from what you carry, and craft devices, weapons, and passives.
  4. Build. Equip skills on the toolbar and passives in your inventory, and combine them into a playstyle.
  5. Push deeper, and the planet pushes back.

Exploration

There is no quest log. The planet's pull draws the Seeker downward, and curiosity does the rest. Hidden rooms sit behind breakable walls, and familiar ground can change while you're away.

The planet is hostile to Seekers. Their Alignment drains whenever they're away from a capsule or the tether network, and once it runs out they start taking damage that grows the longer they stay out. Tether nodes extend that safety outward, so the expedition literally grows from one shelter to the next, and portable tents give a place to rest out in the field.

Death costs what's in your backpack. Everything stored in capsules, along with your skills, passives, weapons, and discovered recipes, carries over.

Combat and builds

Combat is fast and fluid. Attacking is meant to flow with movement rather than interrupt it, and skills are meant to synergize with very few incompatible combinations. The goal is countless distinct playstyles, so players explore the different ways of exploring the world as much as the world itself.

Apart from a small starting kit and crafted devices, every ability is copied from a creature, complete with that creature's animation. Creatures also drop specific materials, so both the abilities and the recipes you want send you to particular parts of the planet, and what you find there opens up others. Fire and ice builds, stealth and backstabs, critical-hit chains, and deployed devices all pull a build in different directions.

Permutations: where it's heading

The long-term direction pushes the loop toward open-ended recursion:

  • Become what you meet. Copying grows from single abilities toward fully becoming a creature for a while, with its movement and senses as well as its attacks.
  • The planet adapts to you. Creatures evolve in response to your playstyle, and their new forms bring new abilities to copy.
  • Materials remember. How you defeat something changes what it leaves behind, and with it what you can craft.
  • The world changes. The caves themselves shift and harden as the planet reacts to the Seeker.

These are directions rather than shipped features. The pages that follow describe where each one stands.

Active Skills

Active skills are everything you trigger yourself: weapon strikes, dashes, spins, deployed devices, and consumables. They sit on the skill toolbar, four slots by default (the Battery Pack passive adds a fifth), and are bound to their own keys or gamepad buttons.

Weapons

The Seeker carries melee and ranged weapons. The sword and hammer are melee weapons with their own reach, weight, and knockback; the hammer hits harder but slows you down. The bow fires arrows, which are a crafted resource. Weapon attacks have directional variants, so striking up, down, or forward while running, jumping, or falling all do what you'd expect.

Abilities copied from creatures

Apart from the starting kit and crafted items, every ability is copied from a creature that uses it. To get a spinning attack, the Seeker learns it from a creature that spins; to get a dash, from one that dashes. A copied ability uses the creature's own mechanic and the creature's own animation: when you use it, you briefly take on that creature's form. The ability can be tuned for the player, with its own damage, timing, and reach, but its look and its origin stay recognizably the creature's.

This ties the toolbar directly to the planet. Every creature you meet is a potential new ability, and building a playstyle means seeking out the creatures that have the abilities you want.

Spit already works this way: the Seeker turns into a spider and spits venom at the nearest foe. Dash Strike copies the bat's dive mechanic to plunge through enemies and burst on landing, but still uses the Seeker's own animation.

The current build still unlocks Dash, Whirl, Stealth, Dash Strike, and Flicker Strike through an experience-based Adaptation system. That system is being removed, and each of those abilities will come from a creature that uses it.

Design principles

  • Flow over interruption. Attacks should blend with movement instead of rooting you in place, unless stopping is the point of the skill.
  • Combine freely. Skills should synergize with each other, with passives, and with weapons, with as few incompatible combinations as possible.
  • Every skill is a playstyle. Stealth rewards patience and backstabs, spinning and flickering attacks reward diving into crowds, and devices reward setting up the battlefield.
  • Every ability has an owner. A new ability starts as a creature. Design the creature that uses it, then let the Seeker copy it.

Where it's heading

As creatures adapt to how you fight, their new forms bring new abilities to copy, so the toolbox keeps growing alongside the planet. Over time, copying should go beyond single attacks toward fully becoming a creature for a while, with its movement and senses as well as its strikes. See Creatures and Ecosystem.

Passive Skills

Passives are always on. Each one changes your stats or the rules of combat under some condition, and each one nudges you toward a particular way of playing. You can equip four at a time.

How passives work today

  • Crafted at capsules. Passives come from capsule recipes, so the materials you collect decide which ones you can make. Seven are craftable so far; the rest are implemented and still being worked into progression.
  • Rolled values. Many passives roll a strength within a range when you get them. Upgrading improves the roll, and its description always shows the current value.
  • Collected, then equipped. Unlocked passives wait in your inventory until you equip them, so you can swap builds back at a capsule.

Design principles

  • Incentivize playstyles. A passive that rewards distance from enemies pushes you toward ranged builds and toward skills that slow or push enemies away. Each passive should suggest a way to play, not just a bigger number.
  • Maximize compatibility. Passives should combine with each other and with active skills as freely as possible. Mutually exclusive pairs, like the two elemental crystals, are rare and deliberate.
  • Combinatorial depth. Picking four from even a modest pool gives thousands of loadouts, before counting skills and weapons. The aim is that a large share of them feel genuinely different.

Where it's heading

The pool keeps growing, and the direction is for passives to tie into the permutation loop: materials that remember how a creature was defeated unlock different recipes, and so different passives. The equipment a Seeker carries ends up recording their relationship with the planet.

Platforming

Movement should feel precise, responsive, and expressive, in the spirit of Hollow Knight. Getting around the planet is meant to be a pleasure in its own right.

Movement today

  • Variable jump height. Release the jump button early for a short hop; hold it for a full jump.
  • Coyote time. You can still jump for a moment after running off a ledge.
  • Wall slides and wall jumps. Slide down walls and kick off them. Touching a wall restores your jump, so you can climb shafts by bouncing between walls.
  • Dash. Bursts you past danger. Like every ability outside the starting kit, it's copied from a creature that uses it. Dash Strike dives straight down through enemies.
  • Extra jumps. The Rabbit's Foot passive adds a double jump.
  • Capsule lifts. Jump inside a capsule and drop through its hatch to ride the lift down.

Spikes hurt and send you back to the last safe ground you stood on, so a mistake costs health and a little time rather than a long walk back.

The caves as a course

Each world is assembled from validated chunk plans, so it's new every time while its routes stay traversable. Broad chambers give room for fights; shafts, ledges, and wall-supported climbs test movement between them. Secret rooms hide behind breakable walls, and the planet can grow new spikes into ground you've already crossed.

Design principles

  • Platforming is optional depth. Difficult platforming should guard hidden areas and rewards, never the main journey. Being good at platforming should pay off without being required to enjoy the game.
  • Movement and combat don't fight each other. You should be able to attack while running, jumping, and falling without losing control of the Seeker.
  • Rhythm. Movement lives on the same musical clock as the rest of the game, so steps, jumps, and landings fall into the soundtrack's pulse.

Where it's heading

Optional platforming challenges are meant to become a bigger part of exploration, guarding hidden areas and rare materials. Learned creature forms should bring their own movement too: becoming a creature can mean moving the way it does.

Creatures and Ecosystem

The planet's caves are full of life that grows stranger with depth. Creatures should feel like part of a living ecosystem rather than a list of enemies, and the planet itself should feel like it's paying attention to the Seeker.

Which creatures appear depends on depth and biome, and every population needs room to live: ground creatures need floor, flyers need open air. Defeated creatures leave materials behind, such as bat wings, and many recipes depend on them, so building toward a device or passive means hunting in the right part of the planet.

Creatures are also where the Seeker's abilities come from. Apart from the starting kit and crafted devices, every ability is copied from a creature that uses it, and plays that creature's animation. A new player ability starts life as a new creature. See Active Skills.

The caves aren't only hostile. Other travelers live down there too: the Vagrant and their yak, the Collector, the Monk, and the Exiled.

The planet's immune response

The planet notices what the Seeker does. Its immunity rises as the Seeker kills and can ease off again, and the HUD shows its current level. As immunity climbs:

  • creature populations grow larger;
  • hidden spike patches start breaking out of the walls, changing ground you've already crossed;
  • at its peak, the Void starts hunting the Seeker.

Every fight changes the world a little. A careful, quiet Seeker meets a different planet from a reckless one.

Ecology: where it's heading

The original design organizes life around ecological roles, giving behavior a reason beyond attacking the player:

  • Herbivores gather at oases where geothermal heat and water let plants grow, eat quickly, and retreat. They fight only to defend themselves, unless they're protecting their young.
  • Carnivores patrol widely and wait near the oases for prey. They spot the Seeker from far away and give chase, and their young hunt alongside them.
  • Scavengers search for leftovers. Alone they flee; in groups, or with young to protect, they grow bold.

Beyond that core sit creatures like bats that attack anything nearby, and trap creatures that wait motionless until something comes close.

The bigger direction is adaptation. Creatures should evolve in response to how the Seeker plays. Lean on fire, and the planet may answer with thicker hides, or with creatures that spit water to put it out. The Seeker can then copy those new abilities in turn, and how a creature is defeated changes what it leaves behind: a bat frozen solid or blown apart should drop something different from one cut down normally. The planet and the Seeker end up shaping each other, round after round.

Actors and actions

This is the design document for enemy and player state machines. The goal is readable, composable behavior with one execution algorithm per mechanic. Small Rust controllers decide what an actor does; reusable actions execute it; typed assets supply the variation. Adding an enemy that combines existing mechanics should require data. A genuinely new mechanic deserves a small, explicit Rust implementation.

Separate decisions from execution

PresentationSprite playback, procedural pose, orientation, and visual effects

Enemy controllers use Combat, Wander, Hopper, or Ambush strategies. Patrol phases and decision timers belong to the controller. Grounded status and collision contacts are physical facts. Playing an animation does not reset decision timing.

Engine code provides playback, collision, and the 96 Hz simulation clock. Game code owns controllers, action definitions, combat policy, and procedural enemy behavior. Keep transitions explicit and ordered; do not require every possible activation condition to be mutually exclusive.

Share mechanics across enemies and the player

An action receives its owner, targeting information, damage attribution, motion parameters, and presentation binding. Its execution should not depend on whether input or AI selected it.

Spider shots and player Spit share projectile execution. Bat dives and player downward dash share lunge execution. Their speed, direction, release timing, cancellation, damage, and artwork can differ through typed definitions. Copying an enemy ability means reusing the mechanic while allowing player-specific tuning. Apart from the starting kit and crafted deployable items, every player ability is copied from a creature: design the creature that uses it first, then bind the player's copy to the creature's mechanic and the creature's animation.

Compose repeated behavior, such as Buzzer's projectile burst, through action parameters. Keep distinct algorithms explicit instead of building a general-purpose gameplay scripting language.

Keep player control responsive

Player locomotion owns idle, running, jumping, falling, and physical contact state. Skill admission owns equipment requirements, input buffering, cooldowns, stock, and sustained-use checks. Toolbar order resolves competing foreground requests; at most one succeeds per tick. Instant uses can succeed alongside it, regardless of their toolbar position.

The foreground slot stores one typed execution. Mechanic-specific ECS tags only index that execution for queries; they do not store another copy of its state. Basic weapon attacks select their weapon and directional variant. Copied abilities bind shared mechanics to their own tuning and presentation. Existing dedicated mechanics use the same foreground lifecycle, so replacing their content does not require changing locomotion.

Locomotion and the foreground skill are separate concerns. Locomotion keeps tracking the appropriate state and background presentation while an action owns selected outputs. Movement curves obey the foreground motion ownership and the player's hit-stop policies. Releasing an action lets presentation select the current locomotion animation without skill-specific restoration code.

The player graphics record a resting anchor independently of an action's temporary offset. Releasing presentation restores that anchor only for the owned execution. Spike recovery suspends motion and action admission under its own lifetime, releases foreground resources, and resumes on a physically supported, hazard-free position after validating the destination again. The recovery anchor and combat's grounded classification have separate meanings.

Passives modify stats and react to combat or skill events through their own systems. Independently living projectiles, deployed objects, and instant skills do not need to occupy the foreground action slot. Shared actions complement the player skill system; they do not require every skill to use the same execution algorithm. Instant placement/payment/effect execution is shared by input admission and Pulse Drive through skills/instant.rs, with an explicit activation origin for their different admission and stealth rules. Tent and tether hold/release previews share skills/placement_preview.rs; their placement evaluators and spawned-object simulations remain separate.

Treat items as independently living effects

An item can spawn immediately or use a foreground deployment action. Instant use spends available backpack stock and creates the object without taking movement or presentation ownership. A deployment can hold the player still, play a pinned once clip, and create the same kind of object at a named release cue.

Deployment validates placement and reserves stock together when it starts. Reserved stock is unavailable to other uses or crafting. Placement is checked again at release. Cancellation before release refunds the reservation; release consumes it exactly once. Completing or interrupting the player action leaves an already deployed object alive under its own lifetime rules. Cooldowns and other activation rules remain at the skill layer; object behavior belongs to the spawned item.

Make ownership and cleanup explicit

Each actor has one foreground action. The action declares which movement axes, facing, and presentation it controls. Resolve death, status effects, knockback, actions, and locomotion through explicit ownership and actor-specific precedence.

Every execution has a unique identity. Completion, cancellation, interruption, and component removal release only that execution's temporary hitboxes and overrides. Cleanup is idempotent. Launched projectiles and other independent effects follow their own lifetimes. Dead or cancelled actions cannot emit stale combat effects.

Use authoritative timing

Decision clocks, action progress, and playback position remain separate. Sprite actions use authored frames, named cues, and once/loop/hold sections. Procedural actions use fixed-tick phases. Both obey the same action lifecycle and ownership contract.

Playback emits ordered start, frame-entry/exit, cue, loop, seek, completion, and interruption events tagged with its execution ID. Consumers use the execution they own. A same-clip restart creates a new execution; holding a frame does not repeat its entry effects. Playback advances at most once per simulation tick, with requests committed at an explicit schedule boundary. Player admission commits before background locomotion and foreground execution; movement is resolved before collision-dependent combat effects. Deferred completion checks the execution identity at commit, so it cannot terminate a replacement action.

Frame-authored walking is local displacement on actual frame entry. Locomotion explicitly selects continuous movement or frame steps. Each step passes through normal tile collision; blocked displacement is discarded. Actor movement uses pixels per tick, while projectile conventions remain explicit at their own boundary. Gameplay facing is world direction; native sprite orientation belongs to presentation.

Procedural presentation does not need a fictitious sprite timeline. Tentacle geometry follows its authoritative fixed-tick pose, with attack timing and hitbox lifetime owned by the action. Void combines multipart presentation with the shared flying pursuit controller. Pursuit approaches a perceived target continuously and uses contact damage and its own contact cooldown, without entering a timeline attack activity.

Make authoring predictable

An enemy definition composes body, perception, controller, action bindings, presentation, stats, and lifecycle policy. Resolve references and typed variants into immutable definitions. Validate required assets, cues, sections, numeric values, and body compatibility before publishing them.

An active action retains its resolved definition and dependencies. Valid edits apply on subsequent activation; looping locomotion adopts edits at a loop or phase boundary. Structural body changes apply on respawn. Invalid edits retain the last valid definition and report a precise error.

Clarity and preserved gameplay feel take priority over minimizing line count. Keep focused tests for timing, cancellation, output ownership, collision, cleanup, and controller decisions. Measure performance with clearly stated workloads and scope.

Timing, audio, and animation

The goal is one musical timeline shared by sound, gameplay, and presentation. Simulation produces timestamped events ahead of playback; the audio mixer places sounds at those sample positions, and rendering presents the corresponding historical poses. Audio buffers and display refreshes consume this timeline at their own rates.

This document describes the current implementation and the contracts to preserve when extending it. Actors and actions explains action decisions and execution ownership; the animation format reference covers asset authoring.

A transport epoch identifies one mapping between device frames and musical time. Resetting that mapping invalidates commands from the previous epoch. Ordinary tick advancement and lead adjustment keep the same epoch.

GameTickPre → GameTickUpdate → GameTickPost executes every admitted simulation tick. Simulation runs ahead of playback so its commands can reach the mixer before their deadlines. The initial lead is four ticks (41.67 ms). Lead grows to cover sustained observed frame intervals, output latency, buffer duration, and a safety tick. It is rounded up to whole ticks and does not shrink during playback. Increasing lead only admits more future simulation ticks: it preserves the audio epoch, active voices, and every scheduled sample timestamp. A late-play diagnostic alone never resets the transport.

The callback publishes coherent bounded snapshots: device position, host playback estimate, mapping, epoch, callback size, latency, and diagnostics. A full snapshot channel evicts an obsolete snapshot. Timestamp regression, implausible latency, and discontinuities fall back to callback arrival plus an estimated buffer of latency. Host-time estimation affects presentation and simulation admission, never scheduled sample positions.

Frame-pacing calibration uses the 90th percentile of a complete 32-sample window. Intervals of 100 ms or more are loading/stall samples and do not enter this estimate. A few irregular startup frames cannot set the persistent lead before the window fills; longer sustained slow rendering can increase it without restarting playback.

Pause and recover explicitly

Short rendering stalls execute the missing simulation ticks. A severe backlog starts an explicit epoch at the next unexecuted tick, silences stale voices, and restores soundtrack phase. It does not increment the gameplay tick past unexecuted work. The host estimate holds while awaiting the callback's epoch acknowledgment. Missing devices use monotonic silent time; device retries create a fresh epoch. SEEKER_SILENT_AUDIO=1 intentionally disables device access.

PauseRequests combines manual P and modal menu, inventory, and capsule-panel ownership before frame clock admission, and derives GameState and GameTransport::paused together. Closing a modal preserves an independent manual pause; the menu's Resume action clears it. Simulation stops admitting ticks, and the mixer freezes music and SFX playheads at the scheduled boundary while continuing to supply silent device buffers. Resume preserves those playheads and replenishes scheduling lead. Menu/loading UI uses real time; gameplay presentation uses PresentationTime. Modal input flushes both gameplay action banks and buffered edges, so resume does not replay menu presses.

Follow one event from gameplay to output

For a globally clocked eight-tick animation starting at tick 5, the first frame enters at tick 5 and the second at tick 8. If the second frame triggers an SFX, its onset is transport sample frame 4,000 at 48 kHz.

  1. GameTickPre → GameTickUpdate → GameTickPost executes tick 8. Script requests commit in ScriptSet::Init; playback advances in ScriptSet::Run and publishes ordered events. Cues, collision poses, and frame-driven movement use this authoritative entry.
  2. The script schedules the sound at tick 8. The engine converts that absolute timestamp into a sample position and flushes commands after each simulation tick.
  3. GameTickPost prepares actor and attachment transforms, then captures the tick-stamped sprite and camera poses.
  4. The audio callback applies the sound at sample frame 4,000, even if that position lies in the middle of its buffer.
  5. Rendering selects the historical pose for its estimated display time. The second sprite frame becomes eligible when that cursor reaches tick 8.

The device epoch determines when sample frame 4,000 is heard in wall time. Scheduling lead gives simulation time to produce the event before then. It is not an extra delay added independently to each sound. When rendering catches up through several ticks, every simulation entry still executes even if the display cannot show every intermediate pose.

Prepare assets outside the audio callback

  • The output preference is 48 kHz stereo and 256 frames. Device negotiation can select another supported rate, channel count, sample format, or buffer size.
  • Bevy AudioSource bytes are decoded and resampled on the async compute pool. SFX are prepared before the loading screen completes. Music is prepared on demand; only current, pending, and fading tracks remain decoded.
  • Commands carry an absolute transport frame, epoch, and stable ordering number. Plays, stops, loops, voice gain ramps, and bus gains are applied inside callbacks at their offsets.
  • Late one-shots advance into their remaining tails, or finish silently if their whole duration has passed. Their original onset is never shifted to a buffer boundary.
  • SFX and background voices have separate fixed pools. SFX pressure steals the oldest SFX; it cannot steal music. Background exhaustion is reported, and the music controller can retry.
  • Callback queues, scheduled storage, mixing scratch space, and voice slots are bounded. Decode, file access, logging, and heap allocation remain on other threads. Sample and completion pins outlive callback ownership, including stream shutdown, so the callback does not destroy their final heap allocations.
  • Critical controls have a separate queue. Main-thread pressure or exhaustion of callback control storage requests explicit resynchronization instead of silently abandoning a stop or pause. Completion markers do not depend on an overflow-prone completion queue.

AudioBus::Music, Ambience, and Sfx are independent. Actor scripts retain label/instance ownership. The yak's loop uses the Ambience label and cannot stop the soundtrack.

Give animation an explicit cadence

The engine default frame clock is local; the game installs AnimationClock(FrameClock::Global). Each asset can override it with [settings] frame_clock = "Local" or "Global".

A global animation enters its first frame immediately. Later entries occur on strictly later multiples of its current frame period. An eight-tick animation starting at tick 5 enters its second frame at tick 8, then at 16, 24, and so on. Periods such as 2, 4, 6, and 16 remain valid. An individually frozen animation emits no catch-up entries on thaw; global playback waits for the next strictly later boundary. Holds, seeks, reversal, action ownership, and once-per-tick advancement remain authoritative simulation operations. Common-script tick_quant retains its script-origin meaning.

Local cadence counts ticks from that playback's own frame entries. Global cadence aligns subsequent entries to the shared grid and can shorten the initial frame. Selecting a cadence does not change execution ownership. Common-script origin quantization and animation-frame cadence remain separate settings.

Simulation sprites are the collision contract. AnimationCollider continues to reference the authoritative entity. Frame cues, displacement, collider selection, and audio commands use that tick's frame entry. Render-only proxies never carry actor ownership or colliders.

Present historical poses without extending gameplay ownership

Sprite presentation (engine/src/presentation.rs) keeps up to 64 samples per tracked sprite. Animation/Gent sprites and their sprite children are tracked automatically; other tick-driven sprites use PresentationSource. Each history stores the full sprite pose, visibility, layers, transform, and camera-grid alignment policy. A proxy appears at its birth tick and survives logical despawn until its death tick is presented. App-state exit clears the proxies explicitly.

Keep interpolation and pixel alignment independent

Continuous transforms interpolate between bracketing samples. Sprite atlas frames and visibility step at their recorded boundary. PresentationJump(tick) suppresses interpolation across teleports and frame-step displacement. PresentationPolicy exposes independent pixel_aligned and stepped state overrides. Player graphics remain pixel aligned by default in all existing states. PresentationAnchor gives equipment the same fractional-to-integer anchor correction as its player without changing gameplay parenting.

Interpolation is useful even for a pixel-aligned player: selecting the position between two simulation samples before rounding gives the display the nearest pixel at the requested time. Rounding still produces deliberate pixel steps. Floating positions can be selected by a state policy without changing simulation or audio timing. Disabling interpolation holds the earlier sample until the next boundary; it does not change the animation cadence.

Camera position and shake use the same presentation cursor. Particle emitter descriptors remain game owned while delayed Hanabi instances consume PresentationTime; equipment and attached sprites use historical poses. PresentationAlignment is captured with each sprite pose and copied onto its proxy before game-owned pixel-view alignment. Damage sparks use UprightCameraPixels, retaining camera alignment through logical despawn without recovering markers from a dead source. Foliage shader animation and gameplay visual timers consume presentation time. Display refresh is independent of the musical grid: AutoVsync, pipelined rendering, and a requested maximum frame latency of two remain enabled. Two permits more CPU/GPU overlap; one remains a comparison option for lower display latency. The backend treats this setting as a hint, not a measured display delay.

Give music independent ownership

The music controller (game/src/music.rs) owns the soundtrack independently of animation scripts. Entering gameplay requests track main on the next beat. Track metadata (assets/audio/music/tracks.music.toml) maps this to the existing audio.game.Music / Music3 binding.

The editable metadata includes source sample rate, first downbeat, loop start/end, gain, and beats per bar. The default starts at source frame zero, loops [0, 15_360_000) at 48 kHz (480 beats), and uses gain 0.04. The additional 1,920 silent frames in the source are outside the loop; the file is unchanged. A nonzero first downbeat starts playback at that source offset, skipping an intro.

Game systems use MessageWriter<MusicCommand> to submit these commands (commands below is that message writer):

#![allow(unused)]
fn main() {
commands.write(MusicCommand::Play {
    track: "main".into(), boundary: MusicBoundary::NextBeat,
});
commands.write(MusicCommand::Transition {
    track: "another_track".into(), boundary: MusicBoundary::NextBar,
    fade_bars: 1,
});
commands.write(MusicCommand::SetVolume(0.8));
commands.write(MusicCommand::Stop { boundary: MusicBoundary::NextBeat });
}

NextBeat and NextBar select strictly later boundaries. Immediate selects the current admitted simulation target, which can still be ahead of audible playback. Beat/bar boundaries belong to the global grid. Transitions crossfade for one bar by default; fade_bars is configurable. A track that is not prepared leaves the current track playing and chooses the next eligible boundary once ready. Music volume changes the music bus, including both sides of a crossfade, without disturbing voice envelopes. The default Play also fades in over one bar. Stop uses a short 10 ms fade at its selected boundary. Reconnection schedules a restart on the next beat and seeks to the track's elapsed transport phase; recovery need not be acoustically gapless.

Extend the system at its owning layer

Use GameTime and fixed-tick schedules for gameplay. Use PresentationTime for gameplay visual time, and Time<Real> for menu/loading UI. Rendering may read history but must not emit gameplay cues or decide damage from the displayed sprite.

Add rhythm constants and conversions to the shared grid. Schedule sounds through AudioEngine or the existing script actions; do not add another output stream or a render-frame timer for rhythmic events. Keep stop labels scoped to their bus and execution. Add soundtrack choices to the music library and controller rather than attaching them to an actor animation.

Capture newly introduced tick-driven visuals through PresentationSource or the appropriate particle presentation path. Preserve birth, death, visibility, and discontinuity boundaries. Mark sprite teleports and frame-step movement with PresentationJump; do not assume a component used by one presentation path automatically applies to every other path. A displayed effect may outlive its logical source, but cannot keep that source's gameplay ownership alive.

Keep callback work bounded and retain shared buffers until their last callback reference has been released. Prefer removing measured redundant work over changing cadence, reducing simulation frequency, or sacrificing event ordering. Sprite proxies update render layers directly and avoid unchanged transform/visibility writes.

The shared PixelView derives the presented camera position, physical viewport, integer gameplay scale, raster center, and rounded residual once per render frame. Native pixel meshes use its camera compensation; sprite overlays use camera-relative alignment. The low-resolution pass still rasterizes complete shapes. Preserve the authoring formats

Map PNGs live under assets/levels/maps. A map pixel represents one 96×96-tile chunk: black is an ordinary occupied cell, white selects the unique player-spawn template, and #45ff00 requests geyser content. Transparent cells are empty. Arbitrary rectangular images are cropped to occupied bounds; holes remain holes.

Chunk PNGs live under assets/levels/chunks. Their final underscore suffix declares connectors in U, D, L, and R directions, such as generated_chunk_0042_UDR.png. The runtime indexes connector-compatible templates and their horizontal flips. It exhausts compatible unused templates before deterministic reuse. The spawn template remains unique. Selected pixels compile directly into world arrays without a composite-PNG round trip.

large_chunk_3x3_<number>_<connectors>.png plans are 288×288 tiles. Assembly reserves a complete occupied 3×3 block before assigning ordinary chunks, provided every external neighbor meets the middle cell of an edge and the block contains no spawn cell. Logical slices preserve the plan's continuity, while collision, decoration quotas, adaptations, and rendering keep their existing 96×96 regions.

Generate mosaics from the exposed rock

Carve all startup rooms before running terrain mosaic rules. These rules depend on terrain edges: a room's inner wall and an isolated platform inside it need the same category generation as an outside platform. Clipping categories generated for an unbroken wall cannot create the missing transitions around a new cavity.

Generate the three mosaic masks once for the carved terrain. Each layer consumes the preceding layer's output, and higher layers own overlapping cells. Secret-room covers and optional-spike alternatives reuse this cosmetic structure, clipped to their terrain state. Runtime activation does not rerun mosaic rules.

LDtk selects the actual tiles from a complete category grid. Terrain values are Wall0/base 18, mosaic1 17, mosaic2 19, and mosaic3 20; spikes have their own category. An empty mosaic result uses distance-based categories as a fallback. The category grid and the pixel-distance shading field have different purposes and must not be substituted for one another.

Terrain rule priority is spikes → wall0–wall3 → fog1–fog3 → fog_add. The first priority stage producing a tile wins. Within a stage, authored ordering, probability, allowed flips, and break_on_match control the result. Unresolved cells use catchall tiles discovered from the LDtk data. Tundra grass is evaluated only where the tundra terrain program leaves a cell unresolved. Biome selection happens before per-cell rule evaluation, while matching can still read neighboring values from the full grid.

LDtk pattern 0 ignores a neighbor, positive N requires that value, negative -N forbids it, 1000001 requires any occupied value, and -1000001 requires empty. Out-of-world LDtk samples are empty. Near-background rules evaluate fog transitions before wall catchalls and see all category boundaries in their plane. Background values 1, 2, and 3 describe main/middle/far wall categories; tundra also uses near-wall 4. These categories are not additional parallax planes.

Placement-only requirements do not become cover pixels or collision tiles. A rule with no valid placement is skipped.

The playable geometry, mosaic detail, and exposed-wall shading are prepared at startup. A room's cover is the level as it was before carving, over the room and the border its carving reshades (cover_border_tiles: the reach of edge shading plus a tile). Covers never show: every room stays two borders away from other rooms, optional spikes, and the tiles the intro impact changes. So no carving reaches another room's cover, no cover hides art that changes during play, and each cover matches the ground wherever it ends, whichever rooms are open. Covers are baked with the level's decorations; decorations anchored in a room are drawn on a hidden contents sprite instead of the ground, since they could reach past the thin wall into the entrance. For the same reason, entity placement skips room tiles; rooms hold only their authored ore.

Temporary collision contributions keep a room closed until its entrance breaks. The breakable wall takes five hits; opening removes its wall entity, room cover, and dynamic collision contribution, and reveals the room's contents. Room opening performs bounded entity/collision changes, with no image baking.

Prepare the planet's changes before play

Optional spikes express the planet's increasing hostility and make backtracking surprising. PreparedAdaptation owns stable identity, available states, affected chunks, prepared visual handles, terrain/hazard deltas, and entity activation data. Logical adaptation state is separate from disposable rendering assets.

Startup prepares the initial terrain once and alternative images only for affected regions and their required margins. Nearby spike markers that influence the same surfaces share an activation group. Activation remains one-way, starts at immunity 2, evaluates every 16 game ticks, and waits until the group is outside the player's two-chunk active radius. Disabled hazard entities are prepared with their images.

Activation switches prepared material handles, updates tile state, patches base collision while retaining secret-cover contributions, and enables the hazards before simulation observes the new state. Repeated activation is harmless. No normal activation performs rule matching, texture baking, or a whole-world collision rebuild.

Use this interface for additional adaptations when their behavior fits. An adaptation changing background openings or light coverage needs explicit prepared background/darkness outputs and a publication contract; a generic darkness-patch consumer is not implemented. Prepare only interacting local states, rather than a Cartesian product of every change on the planet.

Bake bounded surfaces and cheap lighting

Render output uses 96×96-tile regions at native artwork resolution. Each region reads enough neighboring input for tile edges, shading, decorations, and blur. Global coordinates control dithering and sampling; internal output borders are not world edges. Up to four Bevy task-pool workers bake independent regions with reusable scratch storage. Ordered logical decisions remain deterministic.

Ground darkening uses Euclidean distance from exposed terrain edges, the established falloff, linear-color attenuation tables, and global-coordinate dithering. Transparent pixels are skipped. The distance implementation is shared with darkness. A ground bake reads an 11-tile input margin for the current shading support. Decorations are indexed to contributing output regions and composited with their existing alpha and placement rules.

The near background, described below, is warped for its parallax depth. Depth biomes receive bounded geyser-radius stamping with the existing inner/outer rings and authored chunk restrictions. Spacing indexes and sparse decoration ownership keep placement checks local.

Gaussian background blur uses linear premultiplied color, horizontal/vertical passes, and one conversion in each direction. Texture gutters and kernel margins preserve continuity. Terrain uses nearest sampling; blurred background textures use their existing filtering. All regional pieces of a parallax plane share a common world center and transform.

The farthest cave plane represents distant light. Openings in nearer cave walls allow that light through; depth and the player's backlight supply the rest of the feel. A baked foreground-darkness layer and inexpensive fullscreen composition express this without dynamic lights or shadow maps.

Darkness consumes compact CPU coverage from unblurred terrain/background artwork, including relevant decoration alpha. The lightmap has two pixels per tile. A pixel is a light source where a camera centred on it would see through the ground and every background layer to the sky; parallax shows a layer at depth d with its content d times nearer the layer's centre. One distance field over the lightmap gives every pixel the light of its nearest source: full at the source, fading with the square of the distance to nothing at 24 world units. Chunks outside the map hold no sources. The bake depends on neither the camera nor the window, and the loading screen waits for it. The result is stored in bounded 2,048-pixel texture-array pages with gutters and an R32Uint page table. No redundant RGBA mask textures or source cameras are needed.

The fullscreen pass darkens by depth below the surface, then baked light and the player's backlight each lift a share of that darkness, and dithering hides 8-bit banding. DarknessSettings in darkness.rs documents the uniform the pass reads.

Follow the artist's cave backgrounds

The artist's hand-made LDtk level defines the look of every background plane. tools/backgrounds/exemplars.py extracts its middle, far, and near IntGrid layers for both biomes into exemplar PNGs: tools/backgrounds/source/exemplars/ for the offline planes and assets/levels/tiles/<biome>/near_bg/exemplar.png for the near plane. Each color is one IntGrid value, black marks the artist's terrain, and transparent cells lie outside the painted area. The artist's project is not part of this repository, but the extracted exemplars are; rerun the extraction only when that reference changes. The same LDtk rules turn these values into tiles, so reproducing the artist's values reproduces the artist's tiles.

Generation separates where a plane opens from how its wall is detailed:

  • Openings. The near plane follows the carved terrain (background_openings.rs): it hugs most terrain surfaces with a skin up to five tiles thick and leaves the rest bare, swells into occasional masses up to about eleven tiles beside the terrain, bridges gaps up to about six tiles wide, and leaves the cave farther from terrain open. Middle and far planes cannot see the terrain: openings.py places the artist's own openings, flipped for variety, in wavy horizontal strata as the artist does. Far openings gather behind middle openings so light reaches through. Strata are laid out in view space and sampled through each plane's parallax depth, so the planes line up wherever the camera stands.
  • Spacing. Walls between neighbouring middle/far openings are at least three tiles thick, openings keep three tiles from the covered area's edge, and every opening is one whole artist opening. Near openings are never one tile wide, near wall never floats or thins to a strand in the air, and near openings smaller than 40 tiles close.
  • Detailing. Image analogies (synthesis.py offline and background_synthesis.rs at runtime) copy each wall cell's value from the exemplar cell whose surrounding walls, openings, and terrain match best. Copies continue from already-written neighbours, so the artist's strokes, clusters, and small openings carry over whole. The near exemplar's context is the artist's near wall tidied by the same rules as generated openings, so synthesis also restores the artist's ragged edges. Detailing reaches up to twelve tiles into middle and far walls, eight into near walls, and two into an opening.

Manual near-background markers add coverage to the generated near wall before detailing; use them for fixups rather than to shape the plane. Ice and tundra share the near openings and differ in their exemplars. Middle/far pairs blend both biomes' openings and artwork by depth in camera space, accounting for each plane's parallax so the background follows the world transition.

tools/backgrounds/generate.py builds three paired variants per map from its footprint, independent of the randomly selected terrain templates. Show the open surface around the spawn

The player-spawn template is an open crater under the sky. surface_backdrop.rs draws the sky, high clouds, distant range, nearer hills, and the crater's far wall as one-chunk-wide images from assets/levels/surface, placed by spawn-chunk rows. Chambers have continuous level floors supported by thick terrain. Irregularity lives in ceilings and side contours; climbs sit beside the chambers and use short, tapered banks attached to the walls. Narrow room mouths preserve those banks, and a horizontal route crossing a shaft has a landing on both sides. The main floors remain clear for ground enemies rather than forming diagonal staircases.

Large-plan validation checks these topology rules, every boundary pixel, the two-tile geometry grid, connected air, and movement both to and from every connector and platform. Keep future changes proportionate

World arrays and the CPU R8 light buffer remain dense. Regional images remove oversized intermediate RGBA surfaces and single-texture dimension limits, but retained assets still grow with the world. Texture residency, eviction, and gameplay streaming are separate work needed for much larger worlds on old laptops.

Useful further improvements should remove concrete repeated work: evaluate alternate terrain rules only near changed cells, reuse precomputed candidate offsets where profiling justifies them, and avoid producing empty region buffers. Secret-cover and spike alternatives still build whole-grid logical tile programs even though their raster output is regional. This is a remaining optimization opportunity, not a reason to introduce a general scheduling or dependency framework now. Preserve numerical/visual comparisons and measure the gain before retaining added complexity.

Scripts

Script assets sequence a fixed set of engine actions: changing slots, spawning scripts, despawning entities, and playing or stopping audio. Animations extend the same format with frame triggers and sprite actions. Use Rust game systems for new mechanics rather than treating asset scripts as a general-purpose programming language.

The game currently ships no standalone .script.toml assets; every .anim.toml animation uses the same triggers and actions. The engine still loads and runs common scripts, as described below.

Create and load a script

Place .script.toml files under assets/. For playback by a dynamic asset key, add a File entry to assets/gameplay.assets.ron:

"script.example.Ambience": File (
    path: "scripts/example/Ambience.script.toml",
),

This is an entry inside the existing RON map, not a complete asset collection. The script file could contain:

[settings]
time_base = "Relative"

[[script]]
run_on_playback_control = "Start"
action = "SlotEnable"
slot = "Ready"

See the format reference for triggers, lifetime behavior, parameters, and actions. Key registration is needed for play_key; direct handles can also be loaded with Bevy's AssetServer.

Start playback from Rust or another script

#![allow(unused)]
fn main() {
use bevy::prelude::*;
use theseeker_engine::script::common::ScriptBundle;

fn start_sequence(mut commands: Commands) {
    commands.spawn(ScriptBundle::new_play_key("script.example.Ambience"));
}
}

Register the system at the intended gameplay boundary and provide entity cleanup when needed. ScriptBundle creates a player, not a rendered sprite. A SpawnScript action creates a separate entity whose lifetime is independent of its caller:

[[script]]
run_on_playback_control = "Start"
action = "SpawnScript"
asset_key = "script.example.Ambience"

Control active playback

ScriptPlayer<T> owns a playback request and its runtime. T is Script for common scripts or SpriteAnimation for animations. Systems can set slots, pause, stop, or request another asset.

#![allow(unused)]
fn main() {
use bevy::prelude::*;
use theseeker_engine::assets::script::Script;
use theseeker_engine::script::ScriptPlayer;

fn mark_ready(mut players: Query<&mut ScriptPlayer<Script>>) {
    for mut player in &mut players {
        if player.current_key() == Some("script.example.Ambience") {
            player.set_slot("Ready", true);
        }
    }
}
}

Slots belong to the particular player/runtime; they are not global flags. A [config] table is parsed into the asset's config field, a map of DynamicConfigValue values. Rust code that needs those values reads them from the loaded asset in Assets<Script> and matches the Int, Float, or String variant; 7 and 7.0 are different variants.

play_key queues a request. ScriptSet::Init commits requests before ScriptSet::Run advances playback in GameTickUpdate. Whether a request starts in the current or a later tick depends on when it was submitted and whether its asset is ready. Until a replacement commits, ordinary slot/config access still addresses the old runtime. Slots set before the first runtime exists are retained for initialization. Actor presentation also has execution-scoped overrides; use that ownership API for actor behavior.

A completed common script stops running, but its entity remains until explicitly despawned. A slot-only script does not remain active indefinitely just because it declares slot triggers. See the reference's lifetime rules before authoring persistent listeners.

Keep timing authoritative

Scripts execute on simulation ticks, not display refreshes. Millisecond and time triggers use simulation ClockTime; scheduled audio preserves their timestamp even though Rust dispatch happens during a tick. Use run_on_playback_control = "Start" for immediate initialization. The current nonperiodic tick/time trigger checks are strictly past the requested boundary; run_at_tick = 0 runs on the following tick.

Use animation frame cues for frame-owned effects and the game music controller for soundtrack transitions. Timing, Audio, and Animation explains the shared transport, pauses, presentation delay, and audio buses.

Script Format Reference

Common scripts are .script.toml assets. Their schema is defined in engine/src/assets/script.rs; execution lives in engine/src/script.rs and engine/src/script/common.rs. Scripts explains registration and playback. Animations inherit these settings, triggers, and actions.

File structure

[config], [settings], and the list of [[script]] entries are optional for a common script. Every action entry needs exactly one trigger, an action name, and that action's required fields. Names and enum values are case-sensitive.

[config]
threshold = 7.0
count = 3
variant = "quiet"

[settings]
time_base = "Relative"

[[script]]
run_on_playback_control = "Start"
action = "SlotEnable"

Entity TimeBase and ScriptTickQuant components override the corresponding asset settings. All time origins use game simulation time, which pauses with the transport, rather than wall time.

Quant strings have the form "N", "N+offset", or "N-offset", with positive N. A pattern matches when (value - offset) % N == 0. A periodic trigger checks only the current absolute tick and does not replay missed matches.

For example, run_every_n_ticks = "16" matches global ticks 0, 16, 32, …; "12+8" matches 8, 20, 32, …. These phases do not restart when the script starts. run_at_time accepts quoted seconds, "minutes:seconds", or "hours:minutes:seconds", with optional fractional seconds; "5:80" is equivalent to "6:20".

Setting an already-enabled slot to true does not trigger it again. Slots are local to the player/runtime. They can be changed by Rust or by the slot actions below.

Use positive delays. A delayed action requires its runtime to remain active until delivery; delay does not extend the lifetime of a common script by itself.

Available actions

SlotEnable, SlotDisable, SlotToggle

Require slot = "Name". Enable, disable, or invert that runtime's boolean slot. Slot transitions can queue further actions during the same script update.

DespawnEntity

Despawns the script's own entity. It takes no parameters.

SpawnScript

Requires asset_key. Creates an independent entity with a common-script player. The caller's despawn does not automatically stop the spawned script.

PlayAudio

Plays a one-shot on the SFX bus. Required: asset_key. Optional: volume (default 1.0), pan (default 0.0; -1 left to +1 right), and label. The runtime chooses from the audio asset binding and tracks the instance under the current script execution.

Audio is prepared outside the callback and scheduled at the action's absolute musical timestamp. A late sound starts inside its remaining tail or is discarded if finished; it is not moved to a buffer boundary. Stop these instances with StopAudio.

PlayBackgroundAudio

Plays on the Ambience bus through the same precisely scheduled custom mixer. Required: asset_key. Optional: volume (default 1.0), loop (default false), and label. This action has no pan parameter. Looping spans the prepared sample; authored music loop/downbeat metadata belongs to the music controller.

[[script]]
run_on_playback_control = "Start"
action = "PlayBackgroundAudio"
asset_key = "audio.example.Ambience"
volume = 0.04
loop = true
label = "RoomAmbience"

[[script]]
run_on_playback_control = "Stop"
action = "StopBackgroundAudio"

The example requires the ambience key to be registered. For the soundtrack, use MusicCommand and tracks.music.toml, described in Timing, Audio, and Animation. Actor ambience and music have separate ownership.

StopAudio, StopBackgroundAudio

Both accept current_script_only (default true) and optional label. StopAudio selects SFX; StopBackgroundAudio selects ambience. Neither stops the music bus.

When current_script_only is true, only instances started by this runtime are candidates. False searches that bus across owners. A label narrows the selection in either case. The stop is timestamped by the executing action. These commands also cancel selected pending starts; ordinary script completion alone does not imply that all its sounds stop.

Lifetime and ordering

Script requests commit in ScriptSet::Init and execute in ScriptSet::Run during GameTickUpdate. Action filters and slots can affect later actions in that update. Entries sharing a nonperiodic timestamp retain their authored order within that trigger list. Do not assume different trigger families form one globally timestamp-sorted script list; the mixer independently orders submitted audio commands by sample position and submission order.

A common script finishes once its nonperiodic tick/time lists are exhausted and it has no periodic tick actions. Slot listeners and queued delays do not keep it alive. For a persistent listener, author an intentional periodic action or use a game-owned lifetime; merely declaring run_on_slot_enable is insufficient. An animation's extended tracker also contributes its playback lifetime.

Normal stop and replacement run "Stop" actions. Despawning the entity directly removes the runtime without promising a final script update, so do not rely on a stop action as the sole cleanup mechanism for resources that must be released on arbitrary despawn. Playback completion itself does not despawn the entity.

Global pause freezes simulation and scheduled gameplay audio together. Individually pausing a script stops its updates but does not freeze every voice it previously started. See the timing architecture for recovery and presentation behavior.

Animations

An .anim.toml asset describes sprite playback, frame cues, and optional script actions. It combines an image, atlas metadata, and a playback definition. The format reference lists the supported fields.

Gameplay uses a 96 Hz simulation clock. An eight-tick frame has a 12 Hz cadence. The game defaults to global cadence: the first frame enters immediately and subsequent frames join the shared grid. These are simulation events; the renderer presents their historical poses with a small delay. See Timing, Audio, and Animation for the transport and presentation design.

Register the image and animation together

Place animations beneath assets/animations/. At startup, game/src/assets.rs recursively scans .anim.toml files and reads their [asset] metadata:

[asset]
key = "anim.example.Pulse"
image = "animations/example/Pulse__r1c4__32x32.png"

[settings]
ticks_per_frame = 8
frame_min = 1
frame_max = 4
frame_start = 1

This example assumes a matching image exists. Its path is relative to assets/. The filename declares one row, four columns, and 32×32-pixel cells. The scanner registers anim.example.Pulse, anim.example.Pulse.image, and anim.example.Pulse.atlas. Use keys = ["anim.example.Pulse", "anim.example.OtherPulse"] instead of key for aliases. New files or registration metadata require rerunning the startup scan.

Frame numbers are one-based, traversing the sheet left to right, then top to bottom. Keep them within the atlas capacity. The image dimensions should match the declared rows, columns, and cell size. Shared images are allowed; do not combine sheets solely to satisfy a naming convention.

Opaque magenta pixels (#FF00FF) in a frame mark its collider. engine/src/assets/sprite_colliders.rs covers the convex hull of each frame's magenta pixels with at most five rectangles and clears those pixels so they never show. Editing a mask or the sheet layout therefore changes combat geometry as well as the artwork.

Choose playback and cues

The required settings above play through once. Add a bounded clip for an explicit loop:

[playback]
start = 1
end = 4
ticks_per_frame = 8
mode = "loop"

Add named sections when one sheet contains several phases. The existing assets/animations/player/skills/DownwardDash.anim.toml shows separate flight and impact sections. Add [[events]] for named gameplay cues, and [[script]] for supported runtime actions such as sound playback or sprite changes. Put combat decisions and damage rules in the owning game action, as described in Actors and Actions.

Do not write an animation-owned atlas index directly from unrelated Rust systems. Use playback requests, sections, and animation actions so collision poses, frame cues, and audio agree. Completion stops playback but does not automatically despawn the sprite or hide its last frame.

Animation Format Reference

An .anim.toml asset extends the common script format. Its schema is in engine/src/assets/animation.rs; playback is in engine/src/animation.rs and engine/src/animation/timeline.rs. Animations explains registration and Rust playback.

File structure

[asset]
key = "anim.example.Strike"

This example requires the declared image to exist. [asset] is read by the game's startup registration scanner; the runtime reads the animation definition. [settings] is required. [config], [playback], [sections.*], [frame_bookmarks], [[events]], [[frame_impulse]], and [[script]] are optional.

Sprite sheet and indexing

The game recursively scans assets/animations/ for .anim.toml files. [asset] supplies one key or a list of keys, plus an image path relative to assets/. The image name must encode atlas dimensions, such as Strike__r1c8__32x32.png for one row, eight columns, and 32×32-pixel cells. Registration creates the animation key and its .image and .atlas keys.

All authored frame indices are one-based; Bevy's atlas index is zero-based. Frames traverse rows left to right, then top to bottom. Frame 1 is the first cell, and zero is invalid. The same atlas can serve several clips. Explicit image_asset_key and atlas_asset_key settings can select other registered assets.

Keep frame ranges, bookmarks, cues, and seeks within the sheet's capacity. Do not depend on malformed input being rejected uniformly: some checks occur during game definition resolution, while generic playback assumes valid data.

Settings

Without a clip override or script seek/loop, playback stops after the terminal frame's duration. It leaves the sprite at that frame; lifetime and visibility belong to the caller.

The game's application policy is global; the generic engine default is local. Global playback enters the first frame immediately, then advances on strictly later global boundaries of the current period. With period 8 and start tick 5, frame entries occur at ticks 5, 8, 16, 24, …. The first frame is shortened. Periods 2, 4, 6, 8, and 16 all remain valid; a six-tick cadence is not forced into a power of two.

Local playback counts each frame's duration from its own entry. An individually frozen global animation emits no catch-up entries and waits for the next strictly later boundary after thawing. Holding a frame does not repeat its entry actions. Display refresh and pixel rounding do not alter this simulation cadence.

Frame bookmarks

[frame_bookmarks] maps names to absolute one-based frames. Each bookmark also emits a cue of the same name when its frame is entered.

A numeric frame with frame_bookmark = "name" is relative to that bookmark:

resolved frame = bookmark frame + numeric frame - 1

[frame_bookmarks]
loop_start = 10
loop_end = 19

[[script]]
run_at_frame = "loop_end"
action = "SetFrameNext"
to_frame_bookmark = "loop_start"

[[script]]
run_at_frame = 4
frame_bookmark = "loop_start"
action = "SetSpriteFlip"
flip_x = true

The second action runs on absolute frame 13. Numeric target 2 relative to that bookmark would select frame 11. to_frame_bookmark overrides the entry's bookmark for SetFrameNow/SetFrameNext; an omitted target index defaults to 1, meaning the bookmark itself.

A string-valued run_at_frame or current-frame condition resolves its named bookmark directly. Bookmark offsets never change run_at_tick or time triggers. Missing bookmarks fall back to frame 1 in generic script frame resolution; author valid names and use game definition validation rather than relying on that fallback.

Named cues

Cues describe frame-owned events for Rust consumers; their emit strings are not commands executed by the animation engine.

[[events]]
on = { frame_enter = 4 }
emit = "Attack"

[[events]]
on = { frame_exit = 6 }
emit = "Recover"

[[events]]
on = { anim_finished = true }
emit = "Finished"

on.bookmark selects frame entry at a named bookmark. If both frame_enter and bookmark are supplied, the explicit frame takes precedence. Entry, exit, and completion selectors can coexist in one event definition; prefer one trigger per entry for clarity. Completion cues run on completion, not interruption or an indefinitely held clip.

Playback publishes ordered start, frame-entry, frame-exit, cue, loop, seek, completion, and interruption events tagged with PlaybackId. AnimationSet::Signals publishes the current tick's events into each animation entity's PlaybackState component after script execution. Consumers must match the playback execution they own. A held frame emits no repeated entry cue, while a real loop or seek to another frame creates new entries. Action cancellation and cleanup remain in game code.

Frame-authored movement

[[frame_impulse]]
frame = 4

y = 0.0

frame, x, and y are required; mode defaults to "once_on_enter", the only supported mode. These are local displacement descriptors for the game's frame-step walking path. They do not move every standalone animation automatically. An actor must select frame-step walking; its resolver can use these entries when explicit walking steps are absent. The owning movement system applies facing, tuning scale, and normal collision policy on actual frame entry.

All common actions are accepted, plus the following animation actions. The examples below are complete action entries to append to an existing animation definition.

Schedules the next frame at the next cadence advance. Optional frame_index defaults to 1; optional to_frame_bookmark selects a destination base. Selecting the current frame creates a hold without repeating frame-entry effects.

[[script]]

[[script]]

Requires ticks_per_frame; optional reset_progress defaults to false. The runtime clamps the new period to at least 1. False limits remaining progress to the new period; true resets the local remaining count. Global cadence still aligns subsequent advancement to the new period's global boundaries.

run_at_frame = 4

reset_progress = true

Music and Tuning

The Seeker's music, sound, animation, and gameplay share one musical foundation: a fundamental of 48 Hz. Use this page when composing music, designing sounds, or tuning anything pitched for the game or for any other work set in its universe.

The tuning rule

A pitch belongs to the Seeker tuning when its frequency is the fundamental multiplied by a ratio of the form

frequency = 48 Hz × X / 2^N        (X and N are whole numbers)

The denominator must be a power of two: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, and so on.

RatioFrequencyValid?
9/854 HzYes: the denominator is 8 = 2³
19/1657 HzYes: 16 = 2⁴
13/878 HzYes
3/272 HzYes
7/484 HzYes
4/364 HzNo: 3 is not a power of two
6/557.6 HzNo: 5 is not a power of two

In other words, every note is a member of the harmonic series of 48 Hz or of one of its lower octaves (24, 12, 6 Hz, and so on), folded into whichever octave you need. Doubling or halving any valid frequency gives another valid frequency, so the rule works in every register.

This is not twelve-tone equal temperament, and it is not a fixed scale: any whole-number X is allowed, so the set of available pitches is open-ended. Higher values of X reach more unusual intervals (11/8, 13/8, 19/16, 21/16), while low ones give familiar consonances (3/2, 5/4, 7/4).

The same rule shapes time

The game's timing follows the same rule, so rhythm, animation, and pitch all share one family:

QuantityRateAs a ratio of 48 Hz
Musical fundamental48 Hz1
Simulation tick96 Hz2
Standard animation12 Hz1/4
Beat (90 BPM)1.5 Hz1/32

Because everything sits on one family of frequencies, sounds triggered by animation and gameplay land on the musical grid. Timing, Audio, and Animation explains how the engine keeps them in sync.

The Seeker tuning file

SeekerTuning48hz.tun is a ready-made keyboard mapping for synthesizers that read AnaMark .tun files (it also contains a VAZ Plus section). It picks six pitches per octave from the rule:

RatioFrequency at 48 HzCents above 48 Hz
1/148 Hz0
9/854 Hz203.9
19/1657 Hz297.5
21/1663 Hz470.8
3/272 Hz702.0
7/484 Hz968.8

MIDI key 12 plays 48 Hz and key 0 plays 24 Hz. Each block of twelve keys repeats the six pitches one octave higher. Several neighboring keys share a pitch, and the second key of each block jumps up an octave, so the layout is meant to be played rather than read as a chromatic scale. You can open and edit it in Scale Workshop.

The file is one selection from the rule, not its limit. Build other selections the same way: choose ratios with power-of-two denominators and anchor them to 48 Hz or one of its octaves.

Composing in the Seeker style

  • Stay in the family. Keep pitched material on valid ratios. Unpitched sounds, noise, and silence are free.
  • Let mathematics shape the music. Phrase lengths, rhythms, and melodies can follow sequences such as primes or the Fibonacci numbers. Listeners shouldn't need to notice; the music should simply feel right.
  • Write for the beat. At 90 BPM, a beat lasts eight animation frames and 64 simulation ticks, so musical events and on-screen motion line up naturally.
